Discussion model / version 5

Does a moving viewpoint change
the exoplanet sky?

Original question: Biswajit Jana's “Finding Earth 2.0” discussion note asks how changing observer–star geometry might change exoplanet detectability over time. This experiment isolates one part: how far the Sun and Earth move away from a fixed spatial reference established in 2026. It is a baseline experiment, not a prediction of new planet discoveries.

What “leave the probe here” means

The cyan reference is fixed at the modeled Earth–Moon barycentre position at epoch 2026.0. It stays at those coordinates in the chosen non-rotating Galactic frame. That is a hypothetical actively held beacon, not a naturally parked spacecraft. A physical release retains Earth's heliocentric and Galactic velocity. The optional orange comparator starts with Earth's modeled heliocentric position and velocity and moves under solar gravity, sharing the Sun's Galactic acceleration. It therefore travels with the Solar System. No Earth gravity, launch, manoeuvre, planetary perturbation, or station-keeping mission is simulated.

Looking back toward that position does not show the year 2026. The distance divided by light speed is about 28 days after 100 years. The readout is an instantaneous geometric light-time estimate; retarded positions and relativistic signal propagation are not solved.

Two timescales, one starting state

The default Milky Way view spans 0–250 million years after the 2026 model origin. The Sun is integrated in the same Galactic potential, using 0.01 Myr leapfrog steps. Gold shows the elapsed Solar path; cyan is the fixed starting reference. The orbital ring is only a radius guide. The optional disk animation rotates the present-day reconstruction as a rigid illustrative pattern at an assumed 20 km/s/kpc; its luminous points are part of this artwork, not independently integrated catalogue stars. Spiral evolution, pattern longevity, star formation and encounters are not predicted.

The 100-year option and both close-up views use a separate, saved 2026–2126 clock. Changing scales pauses playback and preserves each clock. Both begin at J2000 + 26 Julian years, labelled 2026.0; this is not a UTC calendar ephemeris. The JPL planetary approximation is never evaluated millions of years into the future. Earth shares the Solar System marker in the long view, but its individual orbit is not resolved there. At a Galactic radius of 8.2 kpc, 100 years covers only about 0.00017° of the Sun's orbit. That short-interval Milky Way view cannot visibly resolve the drift at true scale. The reference view resolves AU distances with no distance exaggeration. Its grid is parallel to the Galactic midplane and passes through the starting Sun; the actual midplane is 20 pc below it. The Sun-centred view follows the translating Sun. All body markers are enlarged.

Orbits and the Galactic plane

The Sun begins at (0, −8.2, 0.020) kpc with velocity (−233, 0, +7) km/s. The 233 km/s assumption follows the discussion note; the 20 pc height and 7 km/s vertical velocity are illustrative, not a fitted Solar state. Galactic gravity uses the previous model's axisymmetric Miyamoto–Nagai disk, Plummer bulge and logarithmic halo, normalized to 233 km/s circular speed at 8.2 kpc. A leapfrog integrates local displacement in AU with steps no longer than 0.25 year. The hundred-year path is nearly straight; the century experiment does not exaggerate Galactic displacement. The separate long clock compresses millions of model years into seconds of playback.

Mercury, Venus, the Earth–Moon barycentre and Mars use JPL's approximate Keplerian elements, Table 2a, valid over 3000 BC–3000 AD. Earth's globe denotes the barycentre proxy; the Moon and Earth–barycentre offset are omitted. The J2000 ecliptic is rotated through obliquity 23.43928° and the Gaia documentation's ICRS-to-Galactic matrix. A further rotation about Galactic z places the Sun at six o'clock in the map. The ecliptic is inclined about 60.2° to the Galactic plane. The conventional heliocentric Galactic plane and our model midplane are treated as parallel; the small Solar-height orientation correction is omitted.

The optional passive comparator is a massless two-body test particle, integrated by RK4 with 0.001-year steps and 0.01-year output interpolation. Its initial velocity is a central finite difference of the JPL proxy. Solar-system barycentric reflex motion, Galactic tides across its orbit, encounters and measurement errors are omitted. These assumptions are suitable for illustrating inherited motion, not spacecraft navigation.

Spiral arms and winding paths

Spiral arms are concentrations of stars, gas and star formation separated by fainter inter-arm regions, not empty slots cut from the disk. The optional contrast enhancement darkens faint structure in the existing reconstruction and reduces the point-cloud haze; it does not invent arm boundaries. The pattern animation is a schematic rigid rotation, not the differential orbital motion of individual stars.

In Earth & Sun, select “Show Earth’s path through Galaxy” to see the sum of Earth’s orbit and the Sun’s Galactic translation at true AU scale. The orbital plane is oblique to the direction of travel, so this is not a perfect circular helix. A different reference frame changes the spatial shape of the track. These are 3D spatial trajectories parameterized by time, not a simulation of curved spacetime; there is no universal absolute-rest frame. The Sun’s longer path also oscillates vertically in the modeled disk gravity.

New Solar System and gravity views

Sun & all planets combines saved JPL Horizons outer-planet states with approximate inner-planet elements. A separate close-up resolves the Sun’s barycentric reflex motion; the original Earth & Sun view retains its smooth Solar reference. Space & Time displays a softened, logarithmic potential diagram with arbitrary depth. Its numerical readouts describe stationary clocks outside one isolated spherical body, relative to infinity. They do not solve spacetime curvature or calculate actual spacecraft clock corrections. The report gives methods, sampled accuracy checks and tutorials.

What is observation-based?

The 3D display uses soft luminous particles sampled from the reconstruction, a finite disk thickness, an illustrative central bulge and outer warp. These visual depths and the simplified edge-on dust attenuation are artistic parameters, not fitted tomography. The galaxy uses ESA's 2025 Gaia-informed artist's reconstruction, rather than a generic two-arm spiral. It is not a photograph from outside our Galaxy. Texture coordinates are visually registered to the centre and Sun in ESA's labelled map, with an approximate 40 kpc image span and adopted 8.2 kpc Solar radius. Surface brightness supplies colours and sampling density for the 3D display. Added thickness and bulge depth are illustrative. The optional warp uses a sourced amplitude scale and precession rate with an assumed radial profile; it is not recovered Gaia tomography or a dynamically coupled force model. The outer image is masked to exclude the background dwarf galaxy from the disk. No individual rendered point is a catalogue star, and the separate report experiment uses a documented Gaia DR3 catalogue snapshot.

Independent accuracy check

On 23 October 2026, the four planetary approximations were compared with JPL Horizons heliocentric geometric vectors at six exact model epochs: 2026.0, 2026.25, 2051.0, 2076.0, 2101.0 and 2126.0. Maximum sampled 3D differences were about 3,346 km (Mercury), 10,521 km (Venus), 27,885 km (Earth–Moon barycentre) and 159,167 km (Mars). These are sample comparisons, not accuracy bounds over the whole century. They test the planetary approximation, not the Galaxy model or a spacecraft mission. The responses and reproducible comparison are included in the source package.

Questions for scientific review

  • Which frame and observable best isolate observer motion from the target star's own motion?
  • Can a realistic spacecraft trajectory provide a useful baseline beyond existing Earth-orbit and long-term astrometry?
  • For a target's full space motion and planet orientation, when would transit or radial-velocity detectability actually change?

The PDF's example of 30 km/s relative transverse motion at 100 light-years gives about 20.6 arcseconds per century. That is distinct from the Sun's absolute Galactic displacement. A larger baseline alone does not establish a new detection opportunity. The CSV includes annual century coordinates or half-million-year Galactic samples, with assumptions for independent review. Entering “Earth & Sun” selects 0.1 year/second to make orbital motion readable. Faster playback can undersample planetary motion; the selected epoch positions still come from the same equations.
