Report overview

This report investigates a question prompted by curiosity: as the Solar System moves through the Milky Way, could our changing viewpoint help us observe different planetary systems? It describes what the simulation calculates, what the current results mean, and what evidence would be needed for a stronger conclusion. Neighbouring stars move too, so relative motion is central to the question. This project asks how their relative motion changes distances, directions and opportunities to observe exoplanets. We distinguish three questions: how far we move from a fixed spatial reference; how far we move relative to a particular star; and whether the resulting geometry improves a specified detection method. These are different calculations. The demonstration combines a 250-million-year illustrative Galactic orbit model, a published-input Proxima Centauri case study, and a simple transit-geometry experiment. It does not forecast a number of newly discovered or habitable planets. A new Gaia DR3 experiment adds 1,387 selected sources, correlated astrometric errors and a four-scenario comparison of century motion and instrument thresholds. It finds a small, threshold-dependent motion contribution in a standardised planet-injection experiment. This is single-visit accessibility, not a mission discovery forecast.

1. What can already be answered?

Are stars moving with us? Many nearby disk stars share broadly similar Galactic rotation, but not the same three-dimensional velocity. Their relative positions change. A star's own mass does not set its acceleration in the smooth Galactic potential: gravitational and inertial mass cancel in the test-particle equation. Location, velocity, orbit history and the Galaxy's mass distribution matter. Mutual gravity matters for bound systems and close encounters.

Will 250 million years give us a different sky? Yes, but not because we travel through stationary stars. Both observer and targets move. With our assumed 8.2 kpc radius and 233 km/s circular speed, one nominal Galactic period is about 216 million years. After roughly one revolution the Sun is again near the starting azimuth; its neighbours need not be the same. A travelled path length alone cannot tell us which target becomes closer.

Could that help us detect planets? It can change apparent brightness, angular separation and transit alignment. Whether that helps depends on the instrument, observing schedule and system geometry. We can already point telescopes in different directions without waiting for Galactic travel. NASA's exoplanet archive displayed 6,451 confirmed planets, dated 15 October 2026, when checked on 24 October 2026. This is a dated catalogue count, not an estimate of all planets that exist. Kepler's concentrated fields and TESS's much broader sky survey also make it incorrect to treat the catalogue as one fully explored patch of space. [1–3]

Is Proxima approaching? Yes. In our historical-input calculation it is approaching along the line of sight at about 22.2 km/s, while also moving sideways. Its full relative speed is about 32.5 km/s. It does not share exactly the Sun's velocity. The interactive case below shows the distinction.

Would getting closer make a planet habitable? It could improve observations of that planet; it would not by itself establish habitability. A planet's environment depends primarily on its host star, orbit, atmosphere and other physical properties. A habitable-zone classification is not evidence of a habitable surface or life. [4]

2. Reference frames and equations

Let the Galaxy-centred coordinates be non-rotating within the adopted Newtonian model. Subscript S denotes the Sun, E the Earth proxy, and j a target star. Bold symbols are vectors.

robs(t)=rS(t)+rE/S(t),Rj(t)=rj(t)−robs(t),dj(t)=∥Rj(t)∥.\mathbf r_{\rm obs}(t)=\mathbf r_{\rm S}(t)+\mathbf r_{{\rm E}/{\rm S}}(t),\qquad \mathbf R_j(t)=\mathbf r_j(t)-\mathbf r_{\rm obs}(t),\qquad d_j(t)=\lVert\mathbf R_j(t)\rVert .

The main Galactic view cannot resolve Earth's AU-scale orbit inside a kiloparsec-scale scene, so the observer there is the Solar System marker. The close-up uses the Earth–Moon barycentre approximation documented in the model notes.

Vj=r˙j−r˙obs,n^j=Rjdj,d˙j=n^j⋅Vj.\mathbf V_j=\dot{\mathbf r}_j-\dot{\mathbf r}_{\rm obs},\qquad \hat{\mathbf n}_j=\frac{\mathbf R_j}{d_j},\qquad \dot d_j=\hat{\mathbf n}_j\cdot\mathbf V_j .

Negative radial velocity means decreasing distance; positive means increasing distance. The transverse component changes the direction on the sky:

V⊥,j=Vj−(Vj⋅n^j)n^j,dn^jdt=V⊥,jdj.\mathbf V_{\perp,j}=\mathbf V_j-(\mathbf V_j\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_j)\hat{\mathbf n}_j,\qquad \frac{d\hat{\mathbf n}_j}{dt}=\frac{\mathbf V_{\perp,j}}{d_j} .

For a small angular change and nearly constant distance, the scale is:

Δθ≃V⊥,j Δtdj.\Delta\theta\simeq\frac{V_{\perp,j}\,\Delta t}{d_j}.

This expression must use relative transverse speed. Substituting the Sun's 233 km/s Galactic speed would wrongly treat the target as stationary. The original discussion note's example, 30 km/s transverse relative speed at 100 light-years over 100 years, gives approximately 20.64 arcseconds.

Why a common Galactic velocity cancels

If we add the same constant velocity to both target and observer, their relative velocity stays unchanged:

(vj+u)−(vobs+u)=vj−vobs.(\mathbf v_j+\mathbf u)-(\mathbf v_{\rm obs}+\mathbf u)=\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_{\rm obs}.

That is why a large absolute path length need not give a large change in the exoplanet sky. Equal initial velocities at different Galactic positions do not guarantee constant separation forever: their accelerations can differ.

4. What the 250 Myr experiment actually calculates

The Sun and three synthetic, explicitly illustrative targets follow the same smooth axisymmetric potential. The targets are not Proxima, named catalogue stars, or confirmed planetary systems. Their initial offsets and velocity assumptions are supplied in stellar-motion.cjs and data/synthetic-tracers.json.

r¨i=−∇Φ(ri),Φ=Φdisk+Φbulge+Φhalo.\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf r_i),\qquad \Phi=\Phi_{\rm disk}+\Phi_{\rm bulge}+\Phi_{\rm halo}.

The disk is Miyamoto–Nagai, the bulge Plummer, and the halo spherical logarithmic:

Φdisk=−GMdR2+(a+z2+b2)2,Φbulge=−GMbR2+z2+c2,\Phi_{\rm disk}=-\frac{GM_d}{\sqrt{R^2+(a+\sqrt{z^2+b^2})^2}},\qquad \Phi_{\rm bulge}=-\frac{GM_b}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2+c^2}},
Φhalo=vh22ln⁡(R2+z2+rc2rref2),vc2(R)=R∂Φ(R,0)∂R.\Phi_{\rm halo}=\frac{v_h^2}{2}\ln\left(\frac{R^2+z^2+r_c^2}{r_{\rm ref}^2}\right),\qquad v_c^2(R)=R\frac{\partial\Phi(R,0)}{\partial R}.

Here the logarithm's reference scale is 1 kpc and only changes the zero of potential. We adopt disk mass 6.8×10¹⁰ solar masses, a=3 kpc, b=0.28 kpc, bulge mass 5×10⁹ solar masses, c=0.5 kpc and halo core radius 12 kpc. The halo amplitude is calibrated to circular speed 233 km/s at 8.2 kpc. The Sun starts at (0, −8.2, +0.020) kpc with velocity (−233, 0, +7) km/s. These are illustrative assumptions, not a Gaia fit. Coordinates and sign conventions are in MODEL_NOTES.md.

We integrate with a kick–drift–kick leapfrog at 0.01 Myr steps. The exact initial state is shared with the century model. Gold traces the Sun; coloured labelled markers show separately integrated synthetic targets. The sidebar reports separation and relative speed for the selected example. The star's own mass is absent from this external-potential equation. The Galaxy's distributed gravitational field matters; orbiting the Galactic centre is not the same as orbiting a lone central point mass.

5. From changing geometry to detectability

Direct imaging and brightness

For fixed luminosity L and projected planet–star separation a_perp:

F=L4πd2,θsep≃a⊥d,θsep[arcsec]=a⊥[AU]d[pc].F=\frac{L}{4\pi d^2},\qquad \theta_{\rm sep}\simeq\frac{a_\perp}{d},\qquad \theta_{\rm sep}[{\rm arcsec}]=\frac{a_\perp[{\rm AU}]}{d[{\rm pc}]}.

A closer star usually gives more photons and a larger angular planetary separation. It does not automatically improve planet/star flux contrast, since the two fluxes share essentially the same observer distance. A practical model needs instrument throughput, inner working angle, contrast performance, backgrounds, stellar variability and planetary phase.

Transits

Let the planet's orbital angular-momentum unit vector be h and the host-to-observer direction be n. For a circular orbit of radius a, stellar radius R_star and planetary radius R_p:

b=a ∣h^⋅n^∣R⋆,transit geometry possible ifb≤1+RpR⋆.b=\frac{a\,|\hat{\mathbf h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|}{R_\star},\qquad \text{transit geometry possible if}\quad b\leq1+\frac{R_p}{R_\star}.

This is a conjunction geometry test, not a transit light curve or a claim that a transit occurs at the slider's exact time. Orbital phase and an observing window are also needed. For eccentric systems the usual conjunction-distance correction depends on eccentricity and argument of periastron. The toy control uses a circular 1 AU orbit, a Sun-sized host and an Earth-sized planet; it is not a Proxima b orientation model. Its angular half-width is about 0.269°. A change of sightline can move a system into or out of that band. Getting closer alone does not guarantee a transit, and fractional transit depth remains approximately (R_p/R_star)².

Radial velocity and other methods

The Keplerian stellar reflex amplitude is:

K=(2πGP)1/3Mpsin⁡i(M⋆+Mp)2/311−e2.K=\left(\frac{2\pi G}{P}\right)^{1/3} \frac{M_p\sin i}{(M_\star+M_p)^{2/3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}.

It has no direct observer-distance term. Brightness affects measurement precision, while viewing inclination affects the projected velocity. Astrometric reflex angles scale inversely with distance; microlensing instead requires a suitable changing alignment with a background source. A selection function must be specific to the detection method.

Habitability is a separate inference

No control here turns a geometrically accessible planet into a demonstrated habitable world. Proxima b already exists as an observationally detected exoplanet; its habitability cannot be established from our changing distance. It has been independently confirmed with ESPRESSO measurements, and atmospheric survival and stellar activity remain relevant scientific questions. [4,7] This project makes no claim that Proxima b has an atmosphere, liquid water or life.

6. Prior work and the contribution of this project

The broad idea that stellar motion changes transit viewing opportunities is already established in the literature. Kaltenegger & Faherty (2021) propagated nearby stars through a 10,000-year interval to identify changing opportunities to see Earth transit the Sun. That is a reciprocal viewing-geometry study, not a prediction of how many exoplanets our future observers will discover. It is directly relevant precedent. [8]

Bailer-Jones and colleagues (2018) studied stellar encounters with the Sun using six-dimensional Gaia DR2 data, integrating Galactic orbits and propagating correlated measurement uncertainties. That workflow illustrates what a scientifically defensible encounter calculation needs. It is not evidence that our three synthetic targets are actual predicted encounters. [9]

The project began as a transparent educational implementation and a focused community discussion question, originating in Biswajit Jana's supplied note. It connects fixed-reference displacement, relative stellar motion and detection geometry in one inspectable demonstration. No priority claim is made for the general physical principle, and the present code has not established a new planet or a new detection opportunity for a particular real planetary orbit.

7. From the initial demonstration to a catalogue experiment

  1. Select a dated stellar catalogue and documented quality cuts. Obtain positions, parallaxes, proper motions, radial velocities, covariance matrices and binary-system information. Missing radial velocities must not silently become zero.
  2. Transform all stars and the Sun into a consistent frame and epoch. Integrate full systems where required, including Proxima–Alpha Centauri binding. Sample uncertainties and alternative Galactic parameters.
  3. Model evolving Galactic structure, encounters and stellar evolution when the timescale demands them. A static smooth potential can be numerically accurate while physically inadequate over 250 Myr. Do not infer a universal prediction horizon from a small integration error.
  4. Supply planetary orbital orientations, eccentricities and evolution, or an explicitly statistical distribution when those are unmeasured. A sky-direction change alone is insufficient to predict transit entry for an unknown orbital plane.
  5. Define actual instruments and observing schedules. Separate pure geometric gains from improvements in technology, survey coverage, extinction, target availability and selection bias.
  6. Validate against independent astrometry/orbit software and published encounter cases. Report posterior distributions, sample completeness and sensitivity to model choices, not just a single future animation.

Section 11 now implements a first catalogue experiment: a dated nearby-source selection, full five-parameter covariance plus independent RV errors, century propagation, a specified threshold model and paired instrument comparisons. The requirements above remain relevant to a full mission forecast; binary dynamics, observatory scheduling and realistic planetary demographics are not solved by this first experiment. The 250 Myr scene supplies Galactic context; it is not the appropriate precision forecast for Proxima's next encounter.

8. The stationary probe thought experiment

The cyan 2026 marker is a fixed coordinate, initially at the Earth proxy, in the chosen Galactic frame:

rref(t)=robs(0),B(t)=∥robs(t)−rref∥.\mathbf r_{\rm ref}(t)=\mathbf r_{\rm obs}(0),\qquad B(t)=\lVert\mathbf r_{\rm obs}(t)-\mathbf r_{\rm ref}\rVert.

A passive spacecraft inherits its release velocity and accelerates under gravity. Holding it at a fixed coordinate would require cancellation of inherited velocity and continued force compensation. The simulation does not demonstrate that such a mission is feasible. There is no universal absolute-rest location. A useful spacecraft need not remain fixed: New Horizons and Earth measured stellar parallax from separated observing locations. A future probe extension should compare a feasible trajectory and measured baseline against existing astrometry; this project does not supply a propulsion or navigation design.

The reference is a place, not a preserved moment in time. Looking toward it does not show the year 2026 indefinitely. The century view's distance/c readout is an instantaneous geometric light-time scale, not a solution of retarded emission times. Earth plus Solar motion gives a winding spatial path in some frames; it is not a universal helix through spacetime.

9. Verification and reproducibility

Run npm ci, npm run build and npm test using Node.js 22.12 or newer. Build output embeds application assets and pre-rendered mathematical markup; no online library is needed to use the downloaded HTML. data/proxima-input.json preserves the cited input set. stellar-motion.cjs defines its unit conversions, rectilinear propagation, synthetic tracers and transit geometry. stellar-results.json and the exported CSV preserve derived values.

The new research tests also check all catalogue covariance decompositions, source identifier integrity, phase-integrated accessibility, paired epoch-zero controls, threshold monotonicity, warp velocities and exact reproduction of the saved experiment. Tests check astrometric basis reconstruction, radial/transverse decomposition, closest-approach stationarity, common-velocity cancellation, transit boundaries, Galactic integration conservation/convergence and data exports. Existing tests also compare approximate planetary states with archived JPL Horizons samples. These checks establish limited implementation consistency; they do not validate omitted physics or the long-term future of named stars. Detailed historical checks and current results are supplied in AUDIT.md and the validation JSON files.

11. Catalogue experiment: does motion help at an instrument boundary?

Question and contribution

For a fixed nearby-source sample, how does century-scale relative motion change single-visit direct-imaging accessibility, and does that change survive a different instrument threshold? We test this question with a frozen Gaia DR3 sample and paired calculations, instead of converting the Sun's Galactic travel into a discovery count. The deliverable is a reproducible, source-by-source sensitivity experiment, including cases that become less accessible. This is an exploratory contribution, not an established priority claim or a new exoplanet detection.

Data and scope

The ESA Gaia Archive query on 24 October 2026 returned 1,387 sources with parallax at least 50 mas, parallax signal-to-noise greater than 20, a measured radial velocity, RUWE below 1.4 and at least ten visibility periods. The frozen CSV, exact ADQL, retrieval script and checksum are distributed with the code. Identifiers retain their full integer precision. See Gaia DR3 and the data provenance.

The sample is selected, not volume-complete: RV availability and quality cuts exclude many stars and can favour particular stellar types. RUWE filtering does not eliminate all binaries. A Gaia source is not necessarily an independent planetary system. Sources outside the original cut do not enter this fixed-sample experiment. We do not treat the artwork's rendered particles as catalogue data.

We propagate barycentric Cartesian positions at constant relative velocity from J2016.0 to 2026.0 and 2126.0. Radial motion, transverse motion and perspective geometry are retained. This is an instantaneous geometric calculation, not a light-cone or relativistic astrometry solution. Annual Earth parallax, Galactic differential acceleration, binary orbits and stellar variability are omitted. Century-scale application is deliberate; it must not be extrapolated to 250 Myr.

A deliberately controlled planet and instrument model

Each source is assigned the same hypothetical circular 1 AU orbit, Earth radius and geometric albedo 0.3. Orbital orientations and phases are isotropic/random. This standardised injection isolates geometry; it is neither a list of actual planets nor a habitable-zone prescription for different stellar types. It assumes no occurrence rate. For phase angle α\alpha, Lambert reflection and angular separation are:

Φ(α)=sin⁡α+(π−α)cos⁡απ,c(α)=Ag(Rpa)2Φ(α),θ(α)=1000aAUdpcsin⁡α mas.\Phi(\alpha)=\frac{\sin\alpha+(\pi-\alpha)\cos\alpha}{\pi},\qquad c(\alpha)=A_g\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\Phi(\alpha),\qquad \theta(\alpha)=\frac{1000a_{\rm AU}}{d_{\rm pc}}\sin\alpha\ \mathrm{mas}.

The baseline thresholds are 60 mas inner working angle, a contrast floor of 10−1010^{-10}, and limiting planet G magnitude 30. The improved scenario uses 40 mas, 5 × 10−1110^{-11} and G = 31. These are chosen sensitivity settings, not specifications or forecasts for an approved telescope. We assume grey reflection in Gaia's G band. For constant stellar luminosity, stellar magnitude changes by 5log⁡10(dfuture/dinitial)5\log_{10}(d_{\rm future}/d_{\rm initial}), and planet magnitude is stellar magnitude minus 2.5log⁡10c2.5\log_{10}c.

With isotropically distributed planet positions, cos⁡α\cos\alpha is uniform. We solve the simultaneous angular-separation, contrast and magnitude inequalities to obtain the allowed phase interval [αmin⁡,αmax⁡][\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]. The resulting single-visit accessibility is:

Ci=cos⁡αmin⁡−cos⁡αmax⁡2if the interval is nonempty; otherwise Ci=0.C_i=\frac{\cos\alpha_{\min}-\cos\alpha_{\max}}{2} \quad\text{if the interval is nonempty; otherwise } C_i=0.

This marginalisation removes dependence on the absolute sky direction for an isotropic population. It therefore measures distance/brightness and separation-threshold effects, not entry into transit bands for known orbital planes. The two epochs use the same population distribution; we do not claim to follow the orbital phase of an individually known planet.

Paired experiment and uncertainty

Let SS be the sum of CC over the fixed sample. The first index below denotes present/future geometry; the second denotes baseline/improved thresholds:

Δmotion=S10−S00,Δinstrument=S01−S00,I=S11−S10−S01+S00.\Delta_{\rm motion}=S_{10}-S_{00},\qquad \Delta_{\rm instrument}=S_{01}-S_{00},\qquad I=S_{11}-S_{10}-S_{01}+S_{00}.

All four evaluations share the same star and astrometric draw. We use 1,024 draws per source, with a fixed random seed, the full five-parameter Gaia covariance and an independently sampled RV error. The positional RA uncertainty is treated as a tangent-plane quantity, with its cos(dec) factor handled explicitly. The published intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of summed paired differences. They reflect formal input errors under Gaussian assumptions, not total scientific uncertainty. Cross-source correlations, parallax systematics, unresolved binaries, planet-population uncertainty and instrument-model uncertainty are absent. Reproduction is exact for the saved seed; 1,024 draws do not warrant precision in the interval tails.

This counterexample rejects the simple assertion that waiting always improves aggregate accessibility. The negative interaction means the improved thresholds reduce the marginal benefit of motion in this chosen example; it is not evidence that improved instruments perform worse overall.

What this result can and cannot establish

This experiment supplies a real catalogue, explicit selection, consistent short-time propagation, correlated astrometric uncertainty, a specified geometric detection proxy, paired controls, a sensitivity sweep and source-level outputs. An independent numerical integration over phase checks the analytic accessibility formula. Astropy 8.0.2 independently reconstructs the catalogue positions and velocities; propagated distances agree within 7.11 × 10^-15 pc at both epochs. This checks coordinates and units, not the omitted physics. The optional compare-astropy.py benchmark requires Astropy and writes data/astropy-comparison.json. The existing physics tests and a non-default integration-cadence regression test run alongside these checks.

It does not supply a mission yield. Exposure times, throughput, backgrounds, exozodiacal light, scheduling, observatory sky access, repeated visits, planet occurrence, known-planet exclusion and confirmations are not modelled. Applying an arbitrary occurrence factor to S would not solve those omissions. A future mission calculation should use a validated tool such as EXOSIMS, and compare with the instrument-dependent methods of Stark et al..

Research status and the question for the community

The literature search found established studies of proper-motion effects on transits, changing Earth-transit viewpoints, Gaia stellar encounters, and direct-imaging completeness/yields. The sources examined did not establish an identical 2026–2126, Gaia-covariance, four-scenario threshold comparison. This limited search does not prove that none exists. The methods themselves are established; the specific reproducible comparison and source ranking are this project's current contribution. See LITERATURE_SEARCH.md for the dated search scope.

Open question: after modelling binaries, realistic planets, observability and a fixed observing-time budget, does any nearby system retain a scientifically useful century-scale accessibility gain attributable to relative motion, rather than to changed instrument capability—and which prior study already answers this most directly?

12. A warped disk, vertical Solar motion, and planetary paths

The Galactic disk is not perfectly flat. Poggio et al. (2020) inferred a precessing warp with a model-dependent rate near 10.86 km/s/kpc. Poggio et al. (2025) report an outer-disk corrugation in stellar positions and velocities, in addition to the warp. These are evidence for vertical Galactic structure; they do not prescribe a universal corkscrew trajectory for the Sun or every planetary system.

The updated display uses an optional kinematic warp illustration, with a 700 pc height scale at radius 14 kpc and an adopted onset at 8.4 kpc. The onset, radial quadratic profile and initial phase are display assumptions. The surface precesses independently of the spiral artwork. It does not add a new gravitational force to the integrated Solar orbit. The 2025 travelling corrugation is discussed but not fitted or animated as though it were a measured future evolution.

zw(R,ϕ,t)=0.7[max⁡(0,R−8.45.6)]2sin⁡(ϕ−Ωwt) kpc,z_w(R,\phi,t)=0.7\left[\max\left(0,\frac{R-8.4}{5.6}\right)\right]^2\sin(\phi-\Omega_w t)\ \mathrm{kpc},

where R is in kpc, t in Myr, and the signed precession rate in this coordinate convention is −10.86 × 0.001022712 Myr−1\mathrm{Myr}^{-1}. A material tracer following this surface would require:

vz=∂tzw+vR∂Rzw+vϕR∂ϕzw.v_z=\partial_t z_w+v_R\partial_R z_w+\frac{v_\phi}{R}\partial_\phi z_w.

The implementation supplies and tests this derivative. It remains a kinematic description, not a self-consistent evolving gravitational model. Artwork points indicate structure, not integrated physical stars. A dynamically coupled warp requires a different potential and validation against data before being used for predictions.

The Sun already undergoes vertical oscillation in the smooth axisymmetric potential. A new height-versus-time plot makes this motion visible at its true parsec scale without artificially stretching the 3D Galaxy. Select Edge-on, enable Evolving outer-disk warp, and play the million-year clock to distinguish the two motions. Disabling the warp does not change the Solar integration.

In Sun & all planets, all eight planets can be viewed in Solar System coordinates or with Galactic translation included. A separate Solar-wobble close-up resolves the small barycentric reflex motion. Earth & Sun retains a simpler two-body viewing option. The ecliptic is oblique to the Galactic plane. The paths can look winding in Galactic coordinates, while the Sun also has a smaller, non-circular barycentric wobble caused by the planets, and the planets do not trail behind it in a single plane perpendicular to its velocity. A trajectory's appearance depends on the chosen frame. The separate Space & Time view explains weak-field gravity and clock rates using a labelled potential-grid analogy; it does not solve the Einstein field equations.

13. The Sun moves too: eight planets and a shared centre of mass

The Sun is not fixed at the centre of the Solar System. Its planets pull on it, and it moves around the system's barycentre. This is an established effect, explained by NASA's barycentre introduction. The motion is generally a changing loop, not a perfect circular helix. It is also different from the Sun's vertical oscillation around the Galactic midplane.

The new view computes heliocentric positions for all eight planets. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune use saved NASA/JPL Horizons positions and velocities, sampled every quarter Julian year over 2026–2126 and interpolated with cubic Hermite polynomials. The inner four planets retain JPL's approximate Table 2a elements. Thin orbit guides for all planets use approximate elements (including Table 2b corrections for the outer planets); they indicate orbital scale rather than an exact ephemeris track. The Earth marker represents the Earth–Moon barycentre, and giant-planet positions and masses represent their planetary systems. The Solar reflex position is reconstructed using gravitational mass parameters from JPL's DE440/DE441 work:

r⊙,B(t)=−∑iμi ri,⊙(t)μ⊙+∑iμi,ri,B=r⊙,B+ri,⊙.\mathbf r_{\odot,B}(t)=-\frac{\sum_i\mu_i\,\mathbf r_{i,\odot}(t)}{\mu_\odot+\sum_i\mu_i},\qquad \mathbf r_{i,B}=\mathbf r_{\odot,B}+\mathbf r_{i,\odot}.

Here μ=GM\mu=GM, B denotes the barycentric frame, and the sum includes the eight planetary systems. Pluto, asteroids and other small bodies are omitted. This construction preserves the centre-of-mass identity for the included bodies; it does not make the approximate orbital elements a full N-body ephemeris.

Independent numerical check: at six between-node dates per outer planet, the largest differences from separate Horizons requests were 611.1 km for Jupiter, 59.1 km for Saturn, 42.6 km for Uranus and 41.7 km for Neptune. These are sampled interpolation errors, not a proven bound at every date. At six further dates, the reconstructed Sun differed from the Horizons Solar barycentric position by at most 161.8 km (0.024% of a Solar radius). The omitted small bodies and approximate inner-planet inputs remain limitations. Saved requests, values and comparison code are supplied with the project; ordinary use works offline.

Choose Sun's barycentric wobble for a 30-year close-up, with the Sun's radius shown to scale. The other planets lie outside that close-up; the cyan marker is the barycentre. 14. Space & Time: what does a gravity grid mean?

A body does not sit in a literal downward dent in space. The grid in this simulation is an explanatory picture of gravitational potential, drawn as height so that variations are visible. A real relativistic description involves spacetime geometry, with both spatial and temporal components. The connection between gravity and clock rates is experimentally established; see NIST's explanation of atomic-clock tests and Einstein Online.

Outside slowly moving bodies in a weak field, the Newtonian potential and the leading clock-rate terms are:

Φ(r)=−∑iGMi∣r−ri∣,dτdt≃1+Φc2−v22c2.\Phi(\mathbf r)=-\sum_i\frac{GM_i}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i|},\qquad \frac{d\tau}{dt}\simeq1+\frac{\Phi}{c^2}-\frac{v^2}{2c^2}.

The displayed numerical readouts deliberately use one isolated spherical body and a stationary clock, so v=0v=0. The comparison is to a clock at rest infinitely far from that body, with the potential chosen to vanish at infinity. The approximate clock lag per day is therefore:

Δtday≃GMrc2 (86400 s),g(r)=GMr2,r≥Rbody.\Delta t_{\rm day}\simeq\frac{GM}{rc^2}\,(86400\ \mathrm{s}),\qquad g(r)=\frac{GM}{r^2},\qquad r\geq R_{\rm body}.

This does not give the total rate of a real clock on Earth or a moving spacecraft. Those comparisons require the Sun's field, other bodies, rotation, orbital speed, a defined reference clock and propagation effects. Planetary masses and mean radii are sourced from JPL/NASA; the isolated Earth calculation uses Earth's mass alone, excluding the Moon. The giant-planet mass parameters include their moons, an approximation for these educational surface estimates.

The drawn wells use softened distances and a logarithmic height transform to remain visible and finite. That plotted depth has arbitrary units. Changing Visual depth only changes the drawing, never gravity or the clock calculation. The overview projects the moving Solar System into the ecliptic plane; selecting a body opens a normalized local well with horizontal distances in that body's radii. Neither the mesh nor the prescribed trajectories solve Einstein's field equations, light bending or gravitational-wave emission.

The main answer: moving through the Galaxy changes our surroundings over long times, but nearby stars move too. Over a century, the tested instrument improvement changes the selected sample's accessibility far more than stellar motion does. A small subset of targets near a threshold can gain or lose access. The Sun's wobble and the Galaxy's warp are real kinds of motion; adding them to a picture does not by itself imply additional exoplanet discoveries.

These values describe one hypothetical injected planet distribution and instrument setting, not the actual planets present around the selected sources.

The physical scale comparison explains why the Sun can look straight in a short trail. Its century motion spans thousands of AU, while barycentric deviations and the departure from a straight tangent are tiny fractions of an AU. Making those small motions visible requires a close-up or a clearly labelled scale change. All planets inherit the common Solar translation, while retaining different orbital periods and orientations.

16. Are we entering a denser region, and how fast do other stars move?

Two different distances matter. Galactic radius R is the distance from the centre measured in the disk; z is height above its midplane. Moving outward in R generally reduces disk star density. Moving farther from the plane also reduces it, while changing the mixture of thin-disk, thick-disk and halo populations. Neither coordinate alone specifies an individual star's velocity. The displayed synthetic examples A, B and C are invented orbit comparisons, not catalogue stars. They are off by default; only a selected example appears when enabled.

A transparent density scenario

The new panel uses a smooth double-exponential number-density profile, with thin/thick radial lengths 2.6/3.6 kpc, heights 0.3/0.9 kpc and midplane thick/thin ratio 0.12. These structural choices come from the M-dwarf analysis of Jurić et al. (2008). We adopt the project's radius 8.2 kpc and initial height 0.020 kpc, and normalize the profile there:

f(R,z)=exp⁡[−R−8.22.6−∣z∣0.3]+0.12exp⁡[−R−8.23.6−∣z∣0.9],n(R,z)n0=f(R,z)f(8.2,0.020).f(R,z)=\exp\left[-\frac{R-8.2}{2.6}-\frac{|z|}{0.3}\right] +0.12\exp\left[-\frac{R-8.2}{3.6}-\frac{|z|}{0.9}\right],\qquad \frac{n(R,z)}{n_0}=\frac{f(R,z)}{f(8.2,0.020)}.

Reylé et al. (2021) listed 373 stars within 10 pc, including white dwarfs, separately from brown dwarfs and planets. This historical count supplies an illustrative normalization of 0.0890 stars per cubic parsec. It is not a newly measured 2026 census. Applying one tracer population's spatial profile to the whole stellar count is an explicit simplification; the count display is a scenario with no calibrated uncertainty interval.

n0=373(4π/3)(10 pc)3,N10(R,z)≃4π3(10 pc)3n(R,z).n_0=\frac{373}{(4\pi/3)(10\ \mathrm{pc})^3},\qquad N_{10}(R,z)\simeq\frac{4\pi}{3}(10\ \mathrm{pc})^3n(R,z).

Along the current 250 Myr Solar integration, sampled every 0.5 Myr, R stays between approximately 8.1996 and 8.2047 kpc. The Sun does not approach the bulge. Its vertical oscillation changes this smooth density to approximately 0.784–1.063 times the starting value, or 292–397 scenario stars in an instantaneous 10 pc sphere. At the screenshot epoch of +34.9 Myr, the model gives about 0.949 times the starting density. These values do not count new arrivals, discovered planets, or distinct stars encountered over the full journey.

This profile is independent of the force model and the artwork. It omits spiral-arm enhancements, clusters, the bar, halo, warp-relative density changes, stellar evolution and migration. It cannot identify a reliable future arm-crossing date. A denser patch in the picture is not a measured star-count map. The 3–16 kpc comparison range is educational; extrapolation into the bulge is deliberately excluded.

How many stars might enter a neighbourhood?

For a uniform, stationary statistical population, the approximate incoming crossing rate of a sphere of radius b is:

N˙in≃nπb2⟨vrel⟩.\dot N_{\rm in}\simeq n\pi b^2\langle v_{\rm rel}\rangle.

Using the historical normalization, b = 10 pc and an assumed mean relative speed of 30 km/s gives an expectation of approximately 0.086 incoming crossings in 100 years. Similar outgoing flux balances it in this stationary approximation. This is not a prediction of a particular arrival, independent unique targets or new discoveries. The relevant speed is relative to nearby stars; inserting the Sun's 233 km/s Galactic speed would treat those stars as stationary and answer the wrong question.

Speed depends on the potential and the stellar population

The circular speed is a property of an adopted potential, not a universal speed for every star:

vc2(R)=R∂Φ∂R∣z=0,Ω=vcR,T=2πRvc.v_c^2(R)=R\left.\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial R}\right|_{z=0},\qquad \Omega=\frac{v_c}{R},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi R}{v_c}.

A limitation exposed by this comparison: the existing illustrative force model rises outward. It is not a current fit to the observed rotation curve. Põder et al. (2023) inferred 233 ± 7 km/s near the Sun and a curve consistent with flatness across their 5–14 kpc fit. A 2026 Cepheid study reports roughly 236 km/s near the Sun and 230 km/s around 16 kpc, with radial structure. These tracer-dependent results must not be replaced by the model values in the table. Future orbit predictions require a better constrained potential and uncertainty propagation.

Farther out, stars can take longer to go around even when their speeds in km/s are similar: the circumference is larger. Farther above the plane, older populations typically have larger random motions and can rotate more slowly on average. There is no single speed-height law. Our height slider therefore changes density, while the speed readout explicitly remains the midplane circular-speed reference, not the velocity of a star at that height. See the population analysis in APOGEE orbit-superposition work.

Exterior spherical spacetime, with explicit clock references

Space & Time now offers a textured 3D Earth/body, an inverse-square field display, and an exterior Schwarzschild spatial slice. The circular-clock mode includes gravity and ideal orbital motion. Let μ=GM\mu=GM, rs=2μ/c2r_s=2\mu/c^2, and r be the areal radius outside the body's surface. For an isolated nonrotating spherical body:

(dτdt)held=1−2μrc2,(dτdt)circular=1−3μrc2,T=2πr3μ.\left(\frac{d\tau}{dt}\right)_{\rm held}=\sqrt{1-\frac{2\mu}{rc^2}},\qquad \left(\frac{d\tau}{dt}\right)_{\rm circular}=\sqrt{1-\frac{3\mu}{rc^2}},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}.

Here t is the Schwarzschild time referenced to infinity; a held clock needs support. A circular geodesic clock is in free fall even though the displayed Newtonian field magnitude is nonzero. Rates relative to a stationary surface clock use the ratio of the two lapse factors, not the difference of potentials alone. The local animation clock spans 24 hours; its acceleration setting specifies physical seconds per playback second. It is separate from the Galactic and century clocks.

For a spherical, nonrotating Earth, the 400 km circular preset has an approximately 92.4-minute period and −24.7 microseconds/day relative to the stationary surface clock. The 20,200 km preset gives +38.5 microseconds/day. This reproduces the scale and sign of the familiar GNSS result described by NIST; it is not a navigation-grade GPS correction. Earth's rotation, oblateness, other bodies, eccentricity and signal propagation are omitted. See Ashby (2003) for the fuller relativistic timekeeping problem.

The spatial-slice option embeds the constant-time equatorial exterior geometry in Euclidean 3D:

Z(r)−Z(R)=2rs[r−rs−R−rs],1+(dZdr)2=11−rs/r.Z(r)-Z(R)=2\sqrt{r_s}\left[\sqrt{r-r_s}-\sqrt{R-r_s}\right],\qquad 1+\left(\frac{dZ}{dr}\right)^2=\frac{1}{1-r_s/r}.

Only radii outside the physical body are drawn. The embedding height is magnified by the factor shown on screen; the unscaled geometry obeys the radial metric identity. Z is an embedding coordinate, not a physical downward direction. This depicts a spatial slice, not all of spacetime, and marbles rolling on it would not reproduce geodesics. The older softened potential diagram remains a separately labelled analogy. See the derivation in University of Bath relativity notes.
